ForecastHow it works

How each hour and each day gets its rating, and where the model is still a guess.

The short version

Every hour gets a score from 0 to 100. It mostly comes from three things: how warm the rock is, how humid the air is, and how much wind there is. Each rock type weighs them differently. The score then drops to zero if it is raining, if the rock is likely wet, if the air is close to its dew point, or if the rock is far too hot or cold.

A day is rated by its best 3-hour window inside the hours worth climbing at that area. That window is what the forecast names, since it is what you would plan a session around.

The three comfort factors

  • Rock temperature. Air temperature plus up to 8 °C in full sun (8 °C at 1,000 W/m² of sunshine). Full marks between 2 and 12 °C, falling in a straight line to zero at −6 and 24 °C.
  • Humidity. Full marks at or below the rock's "good" humidity, zero at or above its "bad" humidity, a straight line between.
  • Wind. Full marks between 8 and 25 km/h, where a breeze dries skin and rock. Calm air still scores 0.6, and a gale never scores below 0.3: it is unpleasant, not dangerous to the rock.

Fine sandstone with sloping holds loses friction to humidity first, so humidity counts most there. Rough gneiss and basalt still bite in damp air, so temperature counts more.

The gates

  • Rain now. 0.2 mm of rain in the hour, or a 70 % chance of rain, makes the hour score zero.
  • Condensation. When the air is within 4 °C of its dew point the score fades, reaching zero at 1 °C. Cold rock in damp air goes greasy, mostly at dawn.
  • Extreme rock temperature. Past −6 or 24 °C the whole score fades out, reaching zero at −16 or 34 °C. Without this, very dry air could make a scorching afternoon look good.
  • Wet rock. Wet sandstone scores zero. Wet gneiss or basalt keeps 40 % of its score.

Is the rock wet?

A simple bucket model. Each hour, rain fills the bucket, up to 3 mm (the rest runs off), and drying empties it. Drying is faster when it is warm, windy, dry and sunny. The rock counts as wet while there is water in the bucket, and porous rock stays wet for a while after the bucket empties:

The 24 hours for sandstone follows what DAV and IG Klettern ask for at the soft sandstone in Lower Saxony: let it dry for a day after rain. Wet sandstone breaks, and broken holds do not come back. If in doubt, do not climb it, whatever this page says, and follow local guidance.

On a cloudy 10 °C day at 70 % humidity with a 10 km/h breeze, sandstone sheds about 0.16 mm an hour. A full bucket takes about 19 hours, plus the 24-hour soak: roughly two days after a big storm. Sun and wind cut that sharply.

Known limits

  • Grid versus microclimate. The weather model works on cells several kilometres wide. Magic Wood sits in a forested ravine by a river and is probably damper and cooler than its cell.
  • Rock temperature is an estimate. Sun versus shade matters most, and the forecast does not know which way a boulder faces yet.
  • Wetness is a rule of thumb. The page never calls sandstone dry, only "not wet" by the model.
  • Days 5 to 7 are less certain than days 1 and 2, so they are drawn slightly faded.
  • Every number is a first guess. They get tuned against logged sessions: the temperature band first, then the humidity limits, then the sun warming.

Data and privacy

Weather data is by Open-Meteo.com, under CC BY 4.0, from its free non-commercial API. Your browser asks Open-Meteo for the forecast directly, in one request for all six areas, and keeps the answer on your device for an hour so it also works offline. Nothing about you is stored anywhere else.
